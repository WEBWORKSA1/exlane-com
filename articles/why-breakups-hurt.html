Why Breakups Physically Hurt (and What to Do About It)

EL
ExLane Editorial · 7 min read · Updated Sept 2026
Reviewed against published relationship-psychology research; not medical advice.

"It feels like withdrawal" is not a metaphor. Here is what is happening in your body, and why the standard advice (sleep, move, no contact) is actually neuroscience.

1. Reward circuitry

Brain-imaging studies of people who had recently been rejected by a partner found activity in the ventral tegmental area and nucleus accumbens (reward and craving) while looking at photos of their ex. These are the same regions active in cocaine craving. Rejection doesn't switch attachment off; it intensifies it, briefly. This is why the urge to text is strongest in the first days.

2. Stress hormones

Breakups raise cortisol and adrenaline. That produces the chest tightness, racing thoughts at 3 a.m., appetite loss, and "wired but exhausted" feeling. Cortisol also impairs sleep, and poor sleep amplifies emotional reactivity, which produces a loop.

3. Physical pain overlap

Social rejection activates the dorsal anterior cingulate cortex, part of the network that processes the distress of physical pain. In one well-known study, over-the-counter painkillers reduced reported hurt feelings after rejection. (Not a recommendation. It illustrates the overlap.)

4. Identity disruption

Long relationships merge self-concepts: "we" replaces "I" in memory, plans, and habits. Studies show self-concept clarity drops after breakups, and lower clarity predicts more distress. Rebuilding a distinct self is not a cliché; it is the measured mechanism of recovery.
