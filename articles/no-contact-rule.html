No-Contact

The No-Contact Rule: The Complete 30-Day Guide

EL
ExLane Editorial · 9 min read · Updated Sept 2026
Reviewed against published relationship-psychology research; not medical advice.

No-contact is not a trick to get your ex back. It is the fastest known way to get you back. Here is the full protocol.

In this guide
  1. What no-contact is (and isn't)
  2. Why it works: the withdrawal curve
  3. The 7 rules
  4. Day-by-day plan
  5. Exceptions: kids, work, shared leases
  6. If you break it

What no-contact is

No-contact means zero initiated or reciprocated communication with your ex for a fixed period, usually 30, 60, or 90 days. No texts, calls, DMs, likes, story views, "accidental" run-ins, or messages passed through friends. It includes the passive kind: checking their profile, reading old messages, or keeping their location shared.

What it is not: a punishment, a silent treatment, or a strategy to make them miss you. If your goal is to be missed, you are still in the relationship. The goal is to stop feeding an attachment that has no future so it can shrink to a normal size.

Why it works

Romantic attachment runs on the same reward circuitry as other compulsive behaviours. Every contact, even a bad one, is a small hit that resets the withdrawal clock. Brain-imaging studies of recently rejected people show activity in the same regions active in craving. Intermittent contact is the worst pattern of all: unpredictable rewards produce the strongest habits.

Every "just checking in" text restarts the timer. Thirty days of no-contact with one relapse on day 20 is not 30 days. It is 10.

Cravings follow a curve: they peak in the first 72 hours, spike again around day 7–10 and day 21 (often triggered by a memory or a date), then flatten. Most people describe a clear "first okay day" somewhere between day 12 and day 18.

The 7 rules

  1. Mute, don't block-unblock. Blocking then unblocking to check is contact. Mute or restrict, remove from close friends, and turn off suggestions. Block only if they keep reaching out.
  2. Archive, don't scroll. Move photos and chats into an archive folder you can't see. Deleting can wait until you're sure.
  3. No proxies. Don't ask mutual friends how they're doing. Tell friends you don't want updates.
  4. Pre-write the reply. If they text, you don't answer today. If it's logistics, reply once, briefly, and only about logistics.
  5. Replace the ritual. The urge lands at the same times: bedtime, mornings, Friday night. Schedule something for those slots before day 1.
  6. Track it. Use the No-Contact Tracker. Streaks are motivating; relapses are data, not failure.
  7. Tell one person. Accountability roughly doubles completion rates for any behavioural commitment.

Day-by-day plan

Days 1–3: Withdrawal

Expect the worst cravings here. Your only job is not to contact. Sleep, hydrate, and use the Urge SOS timer when your thumb hovers over their name. Delete the thread from your home screen.

Days 4–10: The bargaining window

Your brain will generate excellent reasons to reach out: closure, returning an item, "being an adult". Write them in your journal instead. If an item must go back, mail it or use a friend.

Days 11–20: The first okay days

Energy returns unevenly. Start the 30-Day Glow-Up Challenge now, not on day 1. This is when new routines stick.

Days 21–30: The test

Around week three a trigger usually arrives: a song, an anniversary, a mutual friend's post. Expect it. It passes faster than the first wave. By day 30 you decide whether to extend to 60 or 90 (most people do).

Extend if: you'd still answer their call within a second, you check the counter daily hoping it's "enough", or you're doing no-contact so they'll notice. Relax it if: you feel neutral, you've filled the time, and contact would be a choice, not a need.

Exceptions

Co-parenting: use a "grey rock" version: written, scheduled, child-only communication, ideally through a co-parenting app. Same workplace: professional, brief, no private channels. Shared lease or pets: agree logistics in one conversation, in writing, then revert to no-contact.

If you break it

Log it in the tracker, note what triggered it (time of day, alcohol, loneliness, a photo), restart from day 1, and change one thing about the trigger. A relapse followed by a restart is still progress. A relapse followed by "well, I've ruined it anyway" is the only real failure mode.

If there was abuse or you feel unsafe: no-contact is also a safety plan. Consider blocking everywhere, changing routines, and speaking to a domestic-violence advocate. See the crisis resources in the footer.
